The 20-Minute Limit

The first bit of guidance I give to the interns I hire is, "Don't be stuck for more than 20 minutes. Take 20 minutes to see if you can solve it yourself. After that, ask for help. We're all here to get the job done. You aren't alone. We want you to learn to work independently, but we don't expect you to know how to do that right away. Try being independent for 20 minutes, learn from that, then let someone know." 

20 minutes of wishing you could find the right thing to read or staring into the void hoping for inspiration is plenty. If you have the right buzzwords to feed into Google then you're most likely to find the right article to read in the first few tries. After that the odds drop off; you don't know the right magic words. I bet the success rate for technical questions works like Plank's curve.

If what you really need is inspiration, 20 minutes to summarize what you already know is a good warm-up. If you haven't already thought about the problem and recalled the answer then you're better off either asking for help or setting the problem aside and letting your brain bubble away while you do something else. 

Odds are someone you work with knows the right thing for you to read, can tell you something better to search for, or has already thought through some part of the problem you are working on. They can help you. Investing 20 minutes is enough to ask a good-quality question to get that help. (Less time is probably not enough to ask the good question.)

I expected the interns to struggle with this advice. I had guessed that students would be used to working independently, following academic rules. It was never a problem for them. 

Following this advice was a challenge for more experienced people joining our team, especially when one of them was the team expert on the topic. It's harder to imagine the value of framing the question well, then asking someone less-well-informed for help. I was the tech lead, and - early in the project - I was the only person working on back-end Scala code. When I was clueless, I felt alone. Talking through a problem with our front-end developer, tester, or just someone interested in the project helped me frame the question better to show me what the real concerns were. That at least stopped me "shaving the yak." The Scala community - especially the people who eventually made up the Type Level community - were a huge help getting to concrete answers. (Help from Type Level people almost always starts with, "Oh. See if reading ... helps." It almost always does.)

Later, we hired interns. I found that talking through my problems with an intern was magic. We usually wouldn't come up with a solution right away, but explaining got things fizzing in our heads. I would often make the inspirational connection while I was concentrating on something completely different, like chasing the kids in the park or cooking dinner. It was like my geometry professor from way back warned us: "Keep a notepad on your nightstand. You are going to wake up in the middle of the night with the proof in your head, and you must write it down before you go back to sleep. It'll get better with practice but will never seem natural." 

One of the deeper things in the Toyota Way is that one piece of advice pairs up neatly with another. I think this 20-minute limit pairs well with the first-day plan. It's a small piece of "how-to" to match with the first-day plan's list of "what." If something simple doesn't work smoothly when someone is starting a new job the 20-minute limit sets an expectation for how to solve the problem without giving up the tempo.
