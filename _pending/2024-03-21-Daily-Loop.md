Daily Loop

My team at HMS Catalyst had started with the standard daily scrum stand-up meeting. On days when several of us were busy we used the (newly installed Slack) chat server to just post our status. We started using it more and more, and eventually gave up the meeting. We got to be an example of Maria Chec's "the cool async team."  https://mariachec.medium.com/time-for-a-stand-up-revolution-f690e037e4cd  We switched to chat mostly for convenience, but discovered all sorts of advantages and efficiencies. Some of our staff was overseas. Others had young children. The written progress let them jot down their updates whenever worked best. Anyone - on the team or not - could catch up or trace details by reading for a few minutes. Synchronizing everyone for a meeting meant everyone stopped work, tried to focus on every bit of a conversation, then started work again. The 15-minute meeting took about 45 minutes of productive time. We found the technical staff worked best if each of us had large blocks of uninterrupted time to get a tough problem into our heads and solve it without a mid-morning interruption. 

I have provided written status at every job I have had since my first white collar position at TASC, with one exception. MathWorks didn't require it. However, it's in chapter XX of The Toyota Way, the guide to MathWorks process aspirations. I see people try to hold all this information as oral tradition, struggle, and fail to keep the important details when it is not written down. Writing things down saves so much time, so much coordination cost, and so many misunderstandings. 

Orient (Us) - Roll Up

My next task as tech lead was to digest all the status messages coming in from the team and translate it from the team members' perspectives to a goal-centric view. I do this in the chat editor as I read the individual statuses, as a daily somewhat fat chat message, with a heading break for each goal. That makes the message easy to find and easy to skim. The goals tend to be at the Jira epic  or Jira feature/bug ticket level. I put named praise for people in these whenever I can, and use the same words as we use for tracing work to larger goals to make it easy for my management to follow along if they choose. (I'll link to a future blog entry about tracking and tracing after I publish it.)

Orienting after vacation or business trips took a few minutes reading instead of multiple coordinated conversations over a day or two. Outside observers could read the summary at their convenience, politely meeting the "Only Pigs Can Speak," requirement. https://www.wrike.com/blog/fundamentals-of-the-scrum-methodology/ . 

Orient (So that the stakeholders can observe) - Weekly Roll-Up

On Fridays I cut/pasted/summarized most of a weekly roll-up that I send out on the following Monday. Often the audience for these messages my boss, his boss, and other managers. This summary keeps higher-level leaders aware of how the project is progressing, and provides me easy text for annual reviews for employees and recommendations for our interns. Often this text gets cut into an email; there's usually at least one manager at that level that does not use chat.

Decide - Lead

A lot of being a tech lead is noticing when someone on the team needs help, then making sure that the help solves the problem and maybe helps learn something. Setting a quick, observable tempo (see that future blog entry on tracking and tracing. We use 1-3 day task bites.) plus daily written progress makes it easy to spot places where things weren't coming together as planned. I used private chats or quick conversations over morning coffee to pair up people who were stuck with the people who could help, usually in our pending dedicated office hour. Often I didn't have to say anything; they were already taking care of each other. I haven't had to do much more day-to-day leadership than that. 


Act - Office Hour

To replace post-scrums our team decided to dedicate an hour mid-morning for office hours. We don't schedule any other meetings during this hour so that we're available to talk to each other in ad hoc meetings, usually pair-wise but sometimes the whole team. The set hour gave people time to gather their thoughts before the meeting. This helped make these conversations focus on the problem at hand. Written communication is great for producing a record, but it's not as quick an exchange as a conversation. This also helped new members of the team talk to everyone they work with one-on-one.

Why Not A Scrum Standup?

My teams at HMS Catalyst and BAE started with the standard daily scrum stand-up meeting. I see a lot of popular pushback on scrum. I think scrum is a reasonable starting point for people who have not worked together before. It lets us get at that primate-level of communication, see that we are all working toward a common end, build some trust, and show how each of us contributes to a common goal. However, scrum has these recurring problems: 

 * Scrum is person-focused instead of goal-focused. It is easy to miss lack of progress when a person gives the same status for days on end. 
 * The mechanism of stand-up meetings is built around interrupting all contributors. Even if the meeting is "just 15 minutes long" the total time cost of the meeting is about 45 minutes per person; it takes time to set aside work, mentally prepare the summary for scrum, and - most expensive - get back into the flow after. 
 * If the meeting involves even a modest sized team then every scrum leader struggles to track post-scrums that don't involve them.
 * I have observed managers who attend multiple consecutive stand-ups suffer mental fatigue - even physical fatigue for lack of bathroom breaks. People in recurring meetings are often distracted by phones, laptops, and more important priorities. (Don't blame remote work. People were more distracted back when we stood around in the same conference room.) 
 * The scrum attendees generally fail to record decisions we make. We rely too much on people's implied consensus; people hear what they want to hear, gloss over problems, and fail to remember the details. We address the same questions - often with a different resolution - a few days or weeks later. 
 
Scrum is meant to be a starting point to modify and optimize. It's a solid starting point, easy to optimize to do better with a gradual change to written status. The big thing that written status lacks is the comfort of bringing the team together. We can get together as a group to celebrate our successes instead.
